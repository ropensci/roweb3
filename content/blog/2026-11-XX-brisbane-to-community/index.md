With rOpenSci turning 15 this year, it's incredible to think that I've been lucky enough to be a part of this community for 10 of those years.

While my involvement started in Brisbane in 2016 with the first [AU Unconf](https://ropensci.org/blog/2016/06/16/au-unconf/)[], I had been very aware of the rOpenSci community and it's people and packages.
When rOpenSci was founded in 2011, I had just moved to the Philippines from the US for a job.
Somewhere during the five years that I was living and working in the Philippines became aware of the great work that rOpenSci was doing.
So, while I was aware of rOpenSci, I wasn't sure how I could be involved but I did see the benefit of being involved in the R community and so I joined the Manila R User Group.

## Brisbane 2016 - just the start

Then, it happened.
In 2016 I had just moved to Australia for work and a luck had it, I was in Queensland near enough to Brisbane to attend an rOpenSci Unconf that was being organised by a group of young researchers in Brisbane.
At the same time that I was in Brisbane for the Unconf, my first package, {GSODR} was accepted onto CRAN and that was my quick fact about me when I introduced myself to the other attendees. 
So, while I was an experienced researcher and R user by the time I managed to get involved with rOpenSci, I was still new to developing and publishing R packages.

I was still nervous to attend something like this.
I was new to Australia and new to an event like this.
However, the excitement of bringing Australian R users together to work collaboratively on practical research problems quickly overcame that.
The format of learning by doing and sharing unfinished ideas and building things together really helped build my confidence.
I was one of the few attendees that had knowledge of working with geospatial data and having a deep interest in weather data, I connected with A/Prof Ivan Hannigan.
Ivan had an R package called {awaper} that needed some attention in some areas I knew how to help with.
I also helped create a vignette describing how to work with Australian weather data that greatly influenced a few packages to come.

## From an idea to a package – learning to develop openly

After the great engagement I felt in Brisbane, I went back home and immediately about submitting {GSODR} to rOpenSci for [review](https://github.com/ropensci/software-review/issues/79).
I'd seen how welcoming the community was and I'd seen the value of the peer-review process in my day job but now I saw it with code review as well.
I'd written a few R packages just for internal use before, but nothing like this that was for general consumption.
The feedback I immediately received through the peer review process greatly improved the package just during editorial checks.
Taken together, the editorial comments and the reviewers' comments and suggestions sharpened the package's focus and helped improve reproducibility and the robustness of the package.

The idea for one package, quickly became many.
Having only ever really used R packages, this process helped me understand how to make my work that I shared with other scientists something that they could rely on.
Smoothing the rough edges with data access via an API (or at the time FTP) and wrangling the data into a useable format, *e.g.*, tidy data, made the data more available to other researchers.
But, only if I provided clear documentation and a package that I was willing to maintain as changes occurred in the R ecosystem and with the data itself.

Subsequently, I went on to be involved in developing several other weather and climate related packages that provided access to a myriad of data sources and learned a great deal about APIs and other data formats.
{getCRUCLdata} grew out of scripts I used for my PhD research, {bomrang} grew from the Brisbane Unconf but also from the 2016 GovHack Hackathon where my team won the national Paddock to Plate Award.
The best known of my rOpenSci packages, {nasapower}, grew from work that I started around 2011 and really matured during the review when the POWER team released a proper API.
I rewrote the package from the ground up in the middle of the review process and thanks to that, I learned about interfacing with RESTFUL APIs and how to mock my tests so that I didn't need an Internet connection (shout out to {vcr}).

Since then, most of my packages have focused on making data more accessible to other researchers.
It's gratifying to have a student tell me that they were able to easily get the data that they needed for their research after struggling to find the data and figure out how to access it.

## From contributor to reviewer – giving back

In 2019 I was invited to review packages for rOpenSci and greatly appreciated being given the opportunity to give back to the community.
I reviewed {stats19}, {rnassqs} and {bib2df}.
Reviewing these packages helped me develop a better understanding of how to develop my own R packages and at the same time, allowed me to give back to the community that I had benefited so greatly from.

Then in 2021, Noam Ross invited me to serve as a guest editor for {[allodb](https://github.com/ropensci/software-review/issues/4360)}.
Surprised, but grateful for the opportunity, I accepted.
I stumbled a bit using the rOpenSci bot and handling the submission, but I learned and the package authors learned and ended up with a more robust package.

That experience led to Noam inviting me to join the rOpenSci editorial board, which I gladly accepted.
Being part of the editorial board has allowed me to contribute to open-source initiatives far beyond my own R packages and support the community in so many ways beyond just developing software myself.

I value rOpenSci's way of working and the [Code of Conduct](https://ropensci.org/code-of-conduct/) that so clearly sets expectations.
Being a part of rOpenSci and the editorial board allows me to stay abreast of the changing standards but also to help foster a community that values open-source contributions without being intimidating or inaccessible.
